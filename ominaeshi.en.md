# The Flower Kept Its Honor, the Word Did Not
### How Japanese *jorō* fell from "noble lady" to "whore" — and why the flower named after it didn't

## Start with a word you already know

The English word **hussy** — a coarse word for a disreputable woman — comes from **housewife**. That is not a joke or a folk etymology. The word was worn down in pronunciation and dragged down in meaning at the same time.

Once you notice this, you find it everywhere. **Mistress** was simply the feminine form of *master*: the woman in charge of a household. **Madam** was a term of respect. Both now carry meanings their speakers a few centuries ago would not have recognized.

Japanese has a case that is stranger and more spectacular than any of these. It involves two written characters, a flower that still blooms every autumn, and a poem collection from the 700s. And the twist at the center of it is one that English can't quite produce, because it depends on how Japanese is written.

---

## The problem in one sentence

In Japan today, the word **女郎**, read **じょろう *jorō***, means **prostitute**. In the Edo period a brothel was a *jorō-ya*, and to visit one was *jorō-kai*. In modern Japanese it is a genuinely vulgar word.

But in Japan's oldest poetry anthology, compiled in the 700s, those same two characters **女郎** appear in the names of aristocratic women poets — as a term of *respect*.

And a wildflower called **女郎花** — the same two characters plus "flower" — has been a symbol of elegance and feminine beauty in Japanese poetry for thirteen hundred years, and still is. Nobody blushes at it.

Same characters. Opposite fates. How?

---

## A necessary detour: how Japanese writing works

To follow this story you need one fact about Japanese.

Japanese is written partly in **kanji** — characters borrowed from Chinese. Crucially, Japanese borrowed the *characters*, not the language. A kanji can be read with its imported Chinese-derived sound, or used to write a native Japanese word that already existed and had nothing to do with Chinese.

The consequence is the key to everything below:

> **Two words written with the same kanji are not necessarily the same word.** They may have separate origins, separate histories, and only look identical on the page.

English has nothing quite like this. The nearest analogy is homographs like *bow* (weapon) and *bow* (bend at the waist) — spelled alike, unrelated in origin. Now imagine that happening not by accident but systematically, because the writing system invites it.

Hold on to that. It's the hinge of the whole story.

---

## Layer 1: In the 700s, 女郎 was read *iratsume*

The ***Man'yōshū*** (**万葉集**, "Collection of Ten Thousand Leaves," compiled in the late 700s, roughly 4,500 poems) is Japan's oldest poetry anthology and contains a great many women poets. Their names look like this:

- **Ōtomo no Sakanoue no Iratsume** (大伴坂上郎女)
- **Kasa no Iratsume** (笠女郎)
- **Ishikawa no Iratsume** (石川女郎)

Look at the last two: the characters **女郎** are right there. But they are **not** read *jorō*. They are read ***iratsume***.

***Iratsume*** is a native Japanese word from the archaic period — no Chinese involved. It was an **affectionate honorific for a young woman, especially a noblewoman**. Its masculine counterpart was ***iratsuko*** (郎子). The *Nihon Shoki* (Chronicles of Japan, 720 CE) even glosses the pronunciation explicitly, telling the reader that the characters 郎姫 are to be read *iratsume*.

The characters 郎女, 女郎, 娘子 and others were simply *borrowed* to write this native word. Why those characters? Because in Chinese, 郎 means "young man" and 女郎 (Mandarin *nǚláng*) means "young woman" — the sense in the famous line closing the Chinese "Ballad of Mulan," where her comrades of twelve years never realized she was a woman. The characters fit the meaning, so Japanese scribes used them.

For scale: **Ōtomo no Sakanoue no Iratsume** is the most prolific woman poet in the *Man'yōshū* — 6 long poems, 77 short poems, and 1 *sedōka*, 84 in all, making her the third most represented poet in the entire anthology. She was a central figure of the powerful Ōtomo clan, aunt and mother-in-law to the poet Ōtomo no Yakamochi. Nothing about her was disreputable.

**In the 700s, then: 女郎 = a term of honor for a highborn woman.**

## Layer 2: The flower

In autumn, a yellow wildflower blooms across Japan in fine clusters like grains of millet. Botanically it is *Patrinia scabiosifolia*. In Japanese it is ***おみなえし ominaeshi***.

It is one of the **Seven Herbs of Autumn**, a canonical set fixed by two poems by Yamanoue no Okura in the *Man'yōshū* (Book 8, poems 1537–1538), which name in sequence bush clover, miscanthus, kudzu, pink, *ominaeshi*, boneset, and morning glory.

Fourteen poems in the *Man'yōshū* mention *ominaeshi*. And here is the interesting part: **the spelling 女郎花 did not yet exist.** *Man'yōshū* scribes wrote the name phonetically-cum-semantically in a system called ***man'yōgana***, and they did it in wildly varied ways:

| Spelling in the *Man'yōshū* | What the characters mean |
|---|---|
| 姫部志 | princess |
| 姫押 | princess + press |
| 美人部師 | beautiful person |
| 佳人部為 | fair person |
| 娘子部四 | maiden |

Every single one says *beautiful woman*.

That is because **をみな *omina***, the first element, meant exactly that in old Japanese: a beautiful woman. As for **へし *heshi***, the second element, there are two competing theories and no settled answer:

1. **The "*heshi* = press down" theory** — a flower so lovely it *outdoes* (presses down) a beautiful woman. The spelling 姫押 ("princess-press") fits this.
2. **The "*ominameshi* = women's rice" theory** — millet rice was colloquially "women's rice," and the flower's fine yellow clusters resemble millet grains; *ominameshi* then shifted to *ominaeshi*.

Only in the Heian period (794–1185) and after did the spelling **女郎花** become standard. Note that this is a **Japanese invention**, not a Chinese plant name. In Chinese the plant is 敗醬 — literally "spoiled sauce" — because cut flowers indoors give off a smell like rotting soy sauce. The two cultures looked at the same plant and reached opposite conclusions.

Heian aristocrats adored it and read it as a figure for women and for love. The monk-poet Henjō wrote a famous, gently scandalous poem in the *Kokinshū*: he broke off a stem only because he was charmed by the flower's *name* — so please, he asks, don't tell anyone the monk has fallen. In 898 the retired Emperor Uda even hosted an *ominaeshi* poetry-and-flower contest, where Kijo no Tsurayuki produced an acrostic hiding the five syllables *wo-mi-na-he-shi* at the heads of his lines.

**The flower's name, from the beginning: beauty.**

## Layer 3: The twist — three different words, one spelling

Now the payoff, and the part that requires the fact about kanji.

The tempting story is: *a noble title from the Man'yōshū slowly decayed into a word for a prostitute.* Dramatic, tragic, tidy. **And probably not what happened.**

At least **three separate words** ended up written 女郎:

**① *iratsume*** — the native archaic honorific for noblewomen, described above.

**② *jorō*** — dictionaries generally derive this from a completely different word: ***jōrō* 上臈**. The character 臈 counted the years of training a Buddhist monk had completed, so 上臈 meant a senior monk, and by extension **a person of high rank**. In the Heian court, ladies-in-waiting were ranked *jōrō* (upper), *chūrō* (middle), and *gerō* (lower), so *jōrō* came to mean a high-ranking court lady, then a noblewoman generally. The *Nihon Kokugo Daijiten* states in its note on 女郎 that the word came from 上臈, passing through the written forms 女臈 and 上郎 before settling as 女郎.

**③ *merō*** — from **女童 *mewaraha***, "little girl," which by the Muromachi period had become an insult for women. It parallels ***yarō*** (野郎), originally "young man," now a masculine insult.

So the spelling 女郎 is a **crossroads**: three words with different ancestries met on the same two characters. Modern Japanese *jorō* took route ②. It did not descend directly from the *Man'yōshū*'s noblewomen at all.

One caution, though. The scholar Harashima Yōichi, writing in the *Encyclopedia Nipponica*, points out that even low-grade brothels were written 上らう屋 (*jōrō-ya*) in medieval and early modern texts, and that tying this directly to the imperial court's 上臈 may be **a stretch** — it may simply have been a genteel spelling. So even route ②, the mainstream account, is not airtight.

---

## Layer 4: The Edo period settles it

By the Edo period (1603–1868), *jorō* was fixed as the general term for a prostitute.

The Genroku era (1688–1704) was the transition, with both senses in circulation — ordinary woman *and* prostitute. The 1678 courtesan critique *Shikidō Ōkagami* essentially throws in the towel, observing that since even the lowest women are being called *jorō*, one may as well treat it as the general term for courtesans. By the late 1600s the shift was complete.

Edo brothels had a rigid internal hierarchy — *tayū*, *kōshi*, *sancha*, *hashi-jorō* and others. In Yoshiwara, the Edo pleasure quarter, the highest rank came to be called ***oiran*** (花魁), and *jorō* could then refer to those *below* that rank. In Kyoto and Osaka the general word was *oyama*.

And note that a second word made the identical journey: ***keisei* 傾城**. It comes from a Chinese classical allusion in the *Book of Han* to a beauty so devastating that one glance from her topples a city and a second topples a kingdom — that is, "a woman of ruinous beauty." In early modern Japan, *keisei* meant a high-ranking prostitute.

**"Peerless beauty" → "prostitute." Two words, one road.**

---

## Why does this keep happening?

### Politeness wears out

Japanese linguists describe a **law of diminishing respect** (*keii teigen no hōsoku*): the more an honorific is used, the more its deference erodes, until it slides downward.

The showcase example is ***kisama* 貴様** — literally "honorable one." In the late medieval and early modern period it was genuinely used *upward*, to superiors. By the 1820s it had become an insult. Today *kisama* is close to a fighting word. ***Omae* お前** followed the same arc: it originally meant "before the honorable presence," and now it's a blunt way to address someone below or beside you.

*Jorō* — whatever its exact ancestry — is one instance of this general pattern.

### And words for women slide faster

This is not a Japanese peculiarity. In her 1975 essay "The Semantic Derogation of Woman," **Muriel R. Schulz** argued that words designating girls and women repeatedly begin neutral or even complimentary and then acquire negative meanings over time, while their masculine counterparts frequently do not. *Hussy* from *housewife* is one of her examples; *mistress* against *master* is another; the divergence between *bachelor* and *spinster* is a third.

Japanese supplies its own set — *jorō*, *keisei*, *merō* — arriving at the same place from a completely unrelated linguistic starting point. That convergence is what makes the pattern interesting: it isn't an accident of one language's history.

---

## The asymmetry, and one warning

Here is the part worth sitting with.

The word for the *person* fell. The name of the *flower* did not.

**女郎花 *ominaeshi*** is still one of the Seven Herbs of Autumn, still a stock image in classical and modern poetry, still carrying the sense of feminine beauty that Nara-period poets gave it. Written with the very characters that, applied to a human being, became unusable in polite company.

And precisely *because* of that gap, Edo writers started punning on it — putting the flower and the prostitute in the same line. The comic poet Motono Amiami parodied Henjō's *Kokinshū* poem outright, in an anthology published in 1783: instead of breaking off a beautiful flower and asking that nobody report his fall, his speaker has lice shaken out of him by the wind and asks that nobody report the infestation. Courtly elegance, punctured.

But get the chronology right, because this is where people go wrong: **that pun only became possible in the Edo period.** When Henjō wrote about *ominaeshi* in the ninth century, his joke was about "the flower named beautiful-woman." Prostitution had nothing to do with it. Read his poem with the modern meaning of *jorō* in your head and you will read it exactly backwards.

Which is the real lesson, and it applies far beyond Japanese:

> When you meet a word in an old text, don't ask what it means. Ask what it meant **in the year it was written**.

---

## Caveats

- The derivation of *jorō* from *jōrō* (上臈) is the mainstream dictionary account, but the dictionaries themselves hedge ("perhaps a sound change from *jōrō*"). It is not settled fact.
- The etymology of *ominaeshi* ("press down" vs. "women's rice") remains genuinely unresolved.
- Sources differ on when the spelling 女郎花 became standard, ranging from "after the Heian period" to "mid-Heian."
- Figures such as the 14 *ominaeshi* poems and Sakanoue no Iratsume's 84 poems vary slightly between scholarly editions.
- Japanese and Chinese poems here are described or loosely rendered rather than translated line-for-line; readers wanting the texts should consult a scholarly translation of the *Man'yōshū* or *Kokinshū*.
- Dictionary material (*Nihon Kokugo Daijiten*, *Digital Daijisen*, *Encyclopedia Nipponica*) was consulted via Kotobank. Schulz (1975) appears in Thorne & Henley, eds., *Language and Sex: Difference and Dominance*, Newbury House, pp. 64–75.
